Why This Talk Matters

The reproducibility crisis, shared responsibility, and the cost of bad practices

The Reproducibility Crisis

📉 The Problem

A significant fraction of published biomedical results cannot be reproduced.

This is not just a statistics problem — it is a data practices problem.
Major culprits:
❌ Poor or missing documentation
❌ Lost or overwritten files
❌ Undescribed methods and parameters
❌ Manual, unrepeatable steps
"An article about computational science in a scientific publication is not the scholarship itself, it is merely advertising of the scholarship."
— Buckheit & Donoho, 1995
📰❌🔬

Data Is Only As Good As the Practices Behind It

🗄️

Protect

Raw data is your most valuable asset. Treat it as read-only. Always.
📝

Document

Every manual step that isn't recorded is a potential source of irreproducibility.
🔁

Repeat

The question isn't "does this work?" but "can future me repeat this?"
Good data practices are not about perfectionism — they are about self-preservation

A Peek Into Bioinformatics Work

Data types, assays, pipelines, and how to read the results

What Is Bioinformatics Data?

🧬 What it is

Biological information stored, processed, and interpreted computationally — sequences, counts, intensities, coordinates, clinical variables

📏 The scale

A single RNA-seq experiment can generate billions of reads. Scale is not just a technical challenge — it shapes every analytical decision.
⚠️ Key message: Raw data is not the result — it is the starting point. Processing choices shape every conclusion [1].
📊 Counts — RNA-seq read counts per gene
🔤 Sequences — DNA/RNA base pairs (FASTQ)
📍 Coordinates — Genomic positions (BED, VCF)
💡 Intensities — Microarray signal values
🏥 Clinical — Age, treatment, outcome variables

The Lifecycle of a Bioinformatics Dataset

Every stage introduces decisions that affect downstream results — and the experimentalist owns the first three steps [1]

What to Be Aware of in a Bioinformatics Project

🔀 Batch Effects — Samples processed on different days, machines, or by different people introduce technical variation that can mask or mimic biology
🔢 Sample Size — Underpowered studies are one of the most common and unrecoverable problems in bioinformatics
🏷️ Metadata Quality — Missing or inconsistent sample metadata can make an entire dataset unanalyzable
🗺️ Reference Genomes — Which genome build? hg38 vs hg19 matters. Never assume.
📈 Multiple Testing — Thousands of genes tested simultaneously — correction is not optional
⚠️ Key message: These are not analysis problems — they are design and communication problems [1]

Asking the Right Questions to Your Collaborator

🎯 Before the Experiment

What is the biological question?
What is the hypothesis?
What effect size do you expect?

🧪 About the Samples

How were samples collected, stored, processed?
Are there any known confounders or batch effects?

📦 About the Data

What assay was used and why?
What is the expected sequencing depth?

⚙️ About the Analysis

What tools are being used and why?
What parameters were chosen?

✅ About the Output

What does a positive result look like?
How will results be validated experimentally?
💡 A good question asked before the experiment saves weeks of analysis after it [1]

Questions to Ask When You Receive Results

1
QC: Were any samples or features removed? Why?
2
Statistics: What multiple testing correction was applied? Are effect sizes shown alongside p-values?
3
Parameters: What thresholds were used? Are results sensitive to those choices?
4
Reproducibility: Can this be re-run exactly? Is there a script? [1]
5
Biology: Do the results make biological sense? What is the next experiment?
🔍
Receiving results is not the end of the conversation — it is the middle of it
⚠️ If your analyst cannot answer questions 1–4, that is a reproducibility warning sign

A Typical Bioinformatics Pipeline

📦 Raw Data → ✅ QC → 🗺️ Alignment → 🔢 Quantify → 📊 Statistics → 💡 Interpret

🍳 The Recipe Analogy

Think of a pipeline like a recipe:
Ingredients = raw data
Method = each processing step
Final dish = your results

Changing one step changes the outcome [1]

📝 Why documentation matters

Every step has inputs, outputs, and decisions.

If a step isn't documented, the pipeline cannot be reproduced — even by the person who ran it [1]

The Two Rules That Change Everything

From Noble (2009) — PLoS Computational Biology [1]

Rule 1: Write for a Stranger — Who Is Probably You

"Someone unfamiliar with your project should be able to look at your computer files and understand in detail what you did and why."
— Noble, 2009 [1]
🕵️

The stranger

Is most often you, 6 months from now
🧠

The problem

You will not remember what you did, or why you did it
🛡️

The solution

Good organization is not perfectionism — it is self-preservation

Rule 2: You Will Have to Do It Again

"Everything you do, you will probably have to do over again."
— Noble, 2009 [1]
😩 If disorganized:
Starting over is a nightmare — nothing is findable, nothing is repeatable
😌 If organized:
Repeating it is easy — re-run the script, update the date, done [1]

When does "again" happen?

📦 New data arrives
🐛 A parameter was wrong
📝 A reviewer asks for a different analysis
🤝 A collaborator joins the project
🎓 A new trainee takes over

Organizing Your Project

What good project organization actually looks like — from Noble (2009) [1]

🗂️ A Tale of Two Folders

😱 The Nightmare Folder
project/
├── data.csv
├── data_new.csv
├── data_FINAL.csv
├── data_FINAL_v2.csv
├── analysis.R
├── analysis_copy.R
├── analysis_USETHIS.R
├── Figure1.png
├── Figure1_revised.png
├── Figure1_revised_FINAL.png
└── misc/
    └── stuff/
        └── old/
✅ The Noble (2009) Way [1]
project/
├── data/
│   └── 2024-03-15/
│       ├── counts_raw.csv
│       └── README.txt
├── results/
│   └── 2024-03-15/
│       ├── runall.sh
│       ├── de_results.csv
│       └── figures/
├── doc/
│   └── manuscript/
└── src/
    └── de_analysis.R

📅 Why Date Your Directories?

❌ Logical names fail over time:
analysis_with_batch_correction/

Six months later: Which batch correction? Which samples? Which genome build? [1]
✅ Date-stamped directories tell the story automatically
results/
├── 2024-01-10/  ← pilot analysis
├── 2024-03-15/  ← added new samples
├── 2024-05-02/  ← reviewer response
└── 2025-01-20/  ← updated reference genome
Optionally, add a word or two to indicate the topic — but the date always comes first [1]

The Lab Notebook & Driver Scripts

Documentation habits that make your work reproducible [1]

📓 Your Lab Notebook Lives in Your Results Folder

What to record

✅ Exactly what you did
✅ What you observed
✅ Your conclusions
✅ Ideas for future experiments
✅ Notes from meetings and emails [1]
"Particularly when an experiment turns out badly, document how you know it failed — the interpretation may not be obvious to someone else." — Noble, 2009 [1]

📅 Format

Chronologically organized, dated entries — verbose, not just a figure dump [1]

🛠️ Tools that work well

Quarto document, R Markdown, plain Markdown file, or even a well-structured text file

🤖 The runall Script: Your Experiment in One File

What it is

A single driver script that re-runs your entire experiment automatically — from raw data to final figures [1]

Noble's 5 rules [1]

1
Record every operation
2
Comment generously
3
Never edit intermediate files by hand
4
Use relative pathnames
5
Make the script restartable
# runall.sh
# Date: 2024-03-15
# Purpose: Full DE analysis
# Author: [Your Name]

# Step 1: QC raw reads
if [ ! -f results/qc_done.txt ]; then
  bash src/run_qc.sh
  touch results/qc_done.txt
fi

# Step 2: Align to hg38
if [ ! -f results/aligned.bam ]; then
  bash src/align.sh
fi

# Step 3: Differential expression
Rscript src/de_analysis.R

# Step 4: Summarize results
Rscript src/summarize.R

Version Control: Your Time Machine

Backup, history, and collaboration — Noble (2009) [1]

🕰️ Three Reasons Version Control Saves Lives

💾

1. Backup

A reliable, offsite snapshot of your work at every stage — never lose a working version again [1]
📜

2. Historical Record

Re-run an experiment exactly as it was 3 months ago — just check out that version [1]
🤝

3. Collaboration

Multiple people, same files, no chaos — changes are tracked and merged automatically [1]
💡 Git is to your code what "Track Changes" is to your Word document — but far more powerful

⚠️ What NOT to Put Under Version Control

❌ Do NOT commit:
Large raw data files (FASTQ, BAM)
Automatically generated results
Compiled binaries
Sensitive patient data [1]
✅ DO commit:
Scripts and code
Configuration files
README and documentation
Small metadata files [1]
🔀

💡 The simple rule

If a script generates it, don't track it.
If you type it, track it. [1]

🐛 Handling Errors Without Panic

🔍

1. Detect

Write robust code that checks for bad inputs, missing files, and unexpected values. Use library functions for standard formats [1]
🛑

2. Abort

When an error occurs — stop. Print a clear message. Exit with a non-zero status. "If you skip over errors and continue, you may never notice them" [1]
📄

3. Use Temp Files

Write to results_tmp.csv, rename to results.csv only when complete. Prevents partial outputs from being mistaken for final results [1]
⚠️ A half-written output file that looks complete is one of the sneakiest bugs in bioinformatics — this rule prevents it [1]

📝 Documentation: If It Isn't Written Down, It Didn't Happen

📋 What to document

Software versions, parameter choices, who ran what and when, why decisions were made [1]

📁 README files

A simple README in every project folder goes a long way — one file, plain text, always there [1]

🏷️ Metadata matters

Sample IDs, collection dates, conditions — metadata is as important as the data itself [1]
🎁
Think of documentation as a gift to your future self — and to every collaborator who comes after you [1]

🔁 Reproducibility in Practice

🗄️

Raw Data: Untouchable

Keep raw data read-only. All processing happens in scripts. Never in the original file [1]
📜

Scripts Over Clicks

Prefer scripted workflows over point-and-click tools for any analysis you'll ever repeat [1]
🤝

Share Your Environment

Share code, package versions, and environments when publishing or handing off work
✅ A reproducible analysis: Someone else (or future you) runs your script on your data and gets the same result — every time [1]

Making the Most of Your Results

Productive conversations, the right questions, and red flags to watch for

How to Have a Productive Conversation With Your Analyst

✅ Come with a clear biological question, not just a dataset
✅ Share all relevant metadata upfront — don't assume your analyst knows the experiment
✅ Ask about assumptions, limitations, and what the analysis cannot tell you
✅ Treat it as a dialogue, not a service request
🤝
Collaboration, not transaction

Red Flags in Bioinformatics Results

❌ p-values reported without effect sizes
❌ No mention of quality control steps
❌ Results that seem too clean — biology is messy
❌ Figures with no axis labels or sample sizes
❌ Analysis done in Excel on the raw data file
🚩
⚠️ These are not signs of a bad analyst — they are signs of a communication gap. Ask the questions early, not after submission.
